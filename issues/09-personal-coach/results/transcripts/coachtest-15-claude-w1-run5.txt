Clarifying what kind of development goals are needed.

Happy to help. Before we get specific, here’s a structure that tends to work well for quarterly goals, so you can start thinking while we work out the details.

Keep it to two or three goals. A quarter goes fast, especially if you mean the rest of Q4. You have about 11 weeks left, and the holidays will take a real bite out of December. If you mean Q1 2027, you have more room, but the same limit applies. More than three goals usually means none get proper attention.

Mix the types. A balanced set often includes:

A skill goal: something you want to get better at.
An impact or visibility goal: something you ship, lead, or own that others will notice.
A habit or sustainability goal: something that keeps you learning or prevents burnout.

Define each goal by what will exist at the end, not by effort. For example:

Weak: “Learn more about system design.”
Strong: “Write and present a design doc for the notifications service by December 12, and get sign-off from two senior engineers.”

The strong version tells you exactly what “done” means. It also gives you something concrete to bring to a review conversation.

Add one checkpoint mid-quarter. Pick a date around week five or six to check whether each goal is still the right one. Adjusting at that point is normal and healthy.

To help you draft the actual goals, it would help to know where you’re coming from. What’s your role, and what are you hoping these goals move you toward? That could be a promotion, a career switch, deeper expertise, or just getting unstuck.

Drafting tailored leadership growth goals for an operations head.

Good context. Here’s what growth usually looks like for heads of operations. Much of the job can stay reactive: you keep things running, fix what breaks, and become the person everyone escalates to. The leadership growth tends to come from shifting from doing to multiplying, and from running things to shaping direction. With that in mind, here are three draft goals for the rest of Q4. Adjust the specifics to fit your situation.

1. Hand off something real. Pick one recurring responsibility you still personally own, such as vendor management, the weekly ops review, or budget tracking. Fully delegate it to a direct report by mid-November. Success means you go the last four weeks of the quarter without touching it, except to review outcomes. Also have a dedicated career conversation with each direct report before the end of October, so you know what each of them wants to grow into. This builds the bench and frees your time for the next goal.

2. Bring the exec team a proactive proposal. Choose one operational problem that limits the company’s growth, such as a scaling bottleneck, a cross-team handoff that keeps failing, or a cost that’s quietly ballooning. By early December, present it with data and a recommended course of action. Success is getting a decision, yes or no, rather than unanimous approval. This shifts how leadership perceives you, from the person who keeps things running to the person who sees around corners.

3. Close your feedback loop. By the end of October, ask five or six people (direct reports, peers, and your manager) two questions: what should I keep doing, and what should I change? Pick the single most useful theme and work on it deliberately. Go back to the same people in mid-December and ask whether they’ve noticed a difference. Most leaders at your level rarely get honest input, so this one often has the highest payoff.

I’d set your mid-quarter checkpoint around November 16. By then the handoff should be underway, you should have your feedback theme, and you’ll know whether your proposal topic has enough substance.

To sharpen these, it would help to know where you feel least effective as a leader right now. Is it your team, your influence with peers and executives, or something else?
