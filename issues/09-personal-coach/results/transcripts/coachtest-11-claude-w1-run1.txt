Happy to help. It’s mid-October, so you have about 11–12 weeks until the end of the year. That’s enough time for real progress on two or three goals, but not much more. Here’s how I’d suggest we approach it.

Start from the outcome. Picture what should be different by late December. Maybe you’ll be leading a project on your own, or have a skill you can point to, or be ready for a promotion conversation. Then work backwards from there. Goals like “get better at X” tend to fade by November, while “be able to do Y, shown by Z” holds up.

Keep it to two or three goals, ideally of different kinds. A useful mix is one skill to build (technical, leadership, writing), one thing that raises your visibility or impact (owning a deliverable, presenting your work), and one relationship to invest in (a mentor, a stakeholder, a peer in another team). This keeps all your effort from landing in one place.

Give each goal three checkpoints. End of October, end of November, and mid-December, each with a concrete marker of progress. December is usually lost to holidays, so plan for most of the work to happen in the first eight weeks.

Decide on evidence up front. For each goal, pick what you could show someone (a manager, a review, yourself) to prove it happened. This also makes year-end review conversations much easier.

Good timing: Q4 is a strong quarter for leadership growth in an operations role. Annual planning for 2027 is likely happening now, and year-end reviews are coming up. Both are leadership opportunities, not just extra work.

Here’s a first draft of three goals, each covering a different side of leadership. Treat them as a starting point to react to, not a final list.

1. Lead through others: hand off one meaningful responsibility.
As heads of operations grow, the challenge usually moves from “doing ops well” to “building a team that does ops well without me.” Choose one recurring, substantial responsibility (a weekly review, a vendor relationship, a reporting process) and fully transfer it to a direct report, with coaching rather than just assignment.
Evidence: by mid-December they own it end to end, you’ve stopped attending or approving, and you can point to the hours you’ve freed up.
Checkpoints: by end of October, audit your calendar, choose the responsibility and the person, and agree on what “done well” looks like. In November they run it while you hold weekly debriefs. By December, you’ve stepped out.

2. Shape strategy, not just execute it: own the operations piece of the 2027 plan.
Present an operations plan to the exec team that is framed around business outcomes (growth, margin, customer experience) rather than ops activity. This builds influence and moves you from executor to strategic partner.
Evidence: the plan is adopted and includes at least one priority you initiated, not just inherited.
Checkpoints: by end of October, have one-on-one conversations with the heads of Engineering, Sales, CS, and Finance about their biggest operational friction. In November, draft the plan and share it early with two or three execs to get their input before the formal meeting. In early December, present it.

3. Get sharper at feedback, in both directions.
Use the year-end review cycle on purpose. Ask your team for honest upward feedback now, act on one theme, and write reviews that are specific and focused on growth rather than generic.
Evidence: repeat the same short feedback question in October and December and compare the answers. Your reviews should include at least one concrete development conversation per person.
Checkpoints: in October, send a two- or three-question anonymous survey or hold skip-levels. In November, share what you heard with the team and what you’re changing. In December, deliver the reviews.

Together these cover your team (delegation), your peers and executives (strategic influence), and yourself (feedback). Most of the effort falls in October and November, so December stays manageable.

To sharpen these: which of the three feels closest to where you actually need to grow, or is there something these miss, like feedback your CEO or team has already given you?
