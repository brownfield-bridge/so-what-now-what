Happy to help. Since we’re about ten days into Q4, we can plan either the rest of this year or January through March 2027. The process works the same either way.

Here’s how I’d approach it. First, look back at the last quarter: what went well, what stalled, and what feedback you’ve had from a manager, peers, or yourself. That usually shows where the real growth edges are. Then pick two or three goals at most. More than that tends to spread effort thin, and a quarter goes fast. A good mix is one goal that deepens a core strength, one that closes a gap holding you back, and sometimes one that sets you up for where you want to be in a year or two.

For each goal, make it concrete enough that you’ll know in late December or March whether you hit it. “Get better at public speaking” is hard to measure. “Present at three team meetings and get feedback after each” isn’t. It also helps to tie each goal to a small weekly habit and set a mid-quarter check-in, because most goals don’t fail from lack of ambition. They quietly slip around week five.

Drafting leadership growth goals for an operations head.

Head of operations is a great seat to grow from, because the job sits between strategy and execution. Leadership growth there usually comes down to doing less of the work yourself, building people who can carry it, and shaping decisions above and across your team rather than just executing them.

Here are three goals that tend to matter most in your position. You can adopt them as-is or adapt them.

The first is building leaders beneath you. Many ops leaders stay the person everything runs through, which caps both their team and their own growth. A goal could be: identify the one or two people who could cover for you, hand each of them a meaningful area of ownership by the end of month one, and hold weekly coaching-style one-on-ones where you ask more than you tell. You’ll know it worked if, by quarter’s end, a real problem gets solved well without you in the room.

The second is strengthening your influence at the executive level. Operations is often treated as a support function, and the leaders who break out of that are the ones who bring a point of view, not just status updates. A goal could be: bring one proactive, data-backed recommendation to the leadership team each month, such as a process change, a cost or efficiency opportunity, or a risk you see coming, and ask a trusted peer or your CEO for candid feedback on how you’re showing up in those discussions.

The third is sharpening how you give feedback and lead through change. Operations is constant change management, and how you deliver hard messages shapes trust across the company. A goal could be: run a short anonymous pulse survey with your team now and again in late December, commit to giving specific, timely feedback within 48 hours of noticing something, and practice explaining the “why” behind every significant change you roll out.

I’d pick two of these, not all three, and pair each with a small weekly habit so it survives a busy quarter. A 15-minute Friday reflection on what you delegated, what you’d do differently, and where you added the most value is a simple anchor for any of them.

To narrow this down, tell me where you feel the most friction right now. Is it being stretched too thin, not being heard at the leadership table, team dynamics, or something else entirely?
