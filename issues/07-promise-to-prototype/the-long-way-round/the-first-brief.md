# PROMISE BRIEF — repair or replace

**Built with the promise-builder skill. Interview run 9 September 2026. Answered by Jacek; questions asked cold where the skill requires it.**

> **A promise is the change in the customer's situation that sits between their problem and your solution. It is stated in their words, it is true or false by a date, and they could tell which without being told.**
>
> **You can only promise what you control. Anything else is somebody else's promise, repeated.**

**The problem is theirs. The solution is yours. The promise is the only one of the three that both of you can check.**

---

## THE PROMISE

> ### "I know whether it is worth fixing, instead of guessing."

Said by a householder standing in a kitchen with a repairer's quote in their hand.

**Passes the spoken test** — a person says this sentence out loud, to another person.
**Passes the vocabulary test** — it contains no noun from inside the business.

---

## PART 1 — WHO, AND WHAT IT COSTS THEM NOW

**Q2. Who.** A householder with a large appliance aged roughly five to twelve years, which has a fault, which an independent repairer has just quoted on, and who has to say yes or no today.

Not someone shopping for a new machine. Not someone under warranty. Not someone whose fault is undiagnosed. The entry state is narrow on purpose: **broken, diagnosed, quoted.**

**Market and currency: euro-zone Europe, all amounts in EUR.** Stated here rather than left to an example, because an example is not a specification and should not be read as one.

**Q3. What they do today, and what it costs them.** They search, and get content written by people who sell new machines, plus forum threads about a different model. Many ring a second repairer for a second opinion. Some accept a repair that fails inside a year and pay twice. Some scrap a machine with years left in it.

**Q10. What would make this worse than doing nothing.** Two failure modes, and they are symmetrical: a repair that fails inside a year, so they pay twice; and a machine scrapped while it still had years in it. A tool that only avoids one of them has picked a side, and the business model below is what stops it doing that.

---

## PART 2 — THE PROMISE, AND WHAT MUST BECOME TRUE

**Q5. What must become true.** At the moment of deciding, they can see three things about **this fault, on this model, at this age**: how long it has historically held after repair, what it usually costs, and **whether something else tends to fail soon afterwards** — with the reasoning visible rather than asserted.

The third one is the product. Anyone can divide a €180 quote by a €520 machine. Nobody can currently tell you that on this machine, at this age, with this fault, something else usually goes within a year.

**Q6. Which of the four customer-felt outcomes changes.** **Without you.** The second-opinion call disappears.

**Q4. What they want that we are NOT promising.** Someone turning up today. Scheduling or availability. Any guarantee on the price of the repair. And a replacement finder — ruled out, because it turns the tool into a shopping funnel and breaks the neutrality below.

**Q9. Smallest version worth having.** One category — washing machines. The five most common faults. Three brands. One repairer's records.

---

## PART 3 — THE MODE

**Q7. What was reached for first, answered cold: PREDICT.**
**Q8. What the promise needs: PREDICT, standing on APPLY.**

**No gap.** Which is itself the finding worth recording: run on the same promise two days earlier, the instinct came back *generate* and the requirement came back *predict*. The gap is a property of the person and the moment, not of the domain — and the question earns its place because a promise worded as an experience ("I know…") hides its mode, while a promise worded as a forecast reveals it.

**The prescription question, resolved.** The customer wants to be told what to do, not handed a probability. The tool does both without changing mode: it asks the customer for **their own threshold** — replace above 50% of a new machine, repair at or below 25% — and then tells them which side of their own line this quote falls on. The rule is theirs. The forecast is ours. Nothing prescriptive has been added; a prediction has been placed against a stated rule.

---

## PART 4 — WHAT MUST NEVER BE TRUE

**Q11.** Six, and the last three are the ones that will actually cost money.

1. **Never recommend repair on a safety-related fault.** Safety faults exit the tool immediately.
2. **Never present a confident answer where the data is thin.** Thin data is stated as thin.
3. **Never rank or weight an answer by who pays commission on the outcome.**
4. **Never present a repairer's judgement as an outcome.** An unlogged decline is a missing record, not a machine that did not exist.
5. **Never collect more than the answer needs.** Brand, age, symptoms, quote, decision. No account, no address, no purchase history.
6. **Never use paid or biased opinions from manufacturers or repairers. Records only.**

**On (6).** A manufacturer's stated service life, a repairer's "these last about eight years", a sponsored durability index, a trade-body lifespan table — all are somebody's claim about their own product, and all fail the same test. **What happened is admissible. What someone says usually happens is not.**

It deletes the easy shortcut. The obvious way to fill a thin dataset is to seed it with published average-lifespan figures, and this rules that out — so the tool has less to say on day one and has to say so, which is (2) doing its job at the exact moment it is most expensive.

It also points at us. The tool earns commission on every completed job. A rule that bans paid interests on the input side and says nothing about the output side would be a rule written for other people. So: **the commission is answer-neutral by design, and it is disclosed on the page, not in the terms.**

**Opinion as a test remains admissible.** A repairer looking at five answers and saying which are wrong is falsifying the tool, not feeding it. Judgement as evidence, no. Judgement as a check, yes. That line is thin enough to be worth writing down.

---

## PART 5 — WHAT WE DO NOT CONTROL

**Q17, as found — and it was a veto.** Ten to twenty years of parts, component and failure history across the installed base. Held by manufacturers, who have every reason to keep it. The skill's ruling on a dependency of that shape is **do not build this yet; narrow the promise until it stands on what you control.**

**The narrowing.** One repair business's own job history: what they fixed, on what model, at what age, and what came back through the door. Retrospective, already recorded, no permission needed from anyone but them. **The promise survives smaller and it now belongs to somebody.**

⚠ **Condition.** The repairer must log declines and advised-againsts. Otherwise the sample is pre-filtered by their own judgement and the tool launders an opinion into a statistic — which must-nots 4 and 6 now forbid twice over.

⚠ **Residual, stated not solved.** One repairer's records are one business's customers, one town, one set of brands they happen to service. This is not a representative sample and is not described as one.

**The second source, and it is ours: the intake gate.** Every user, before they get an answer, supplies brand, age and symptoms — and afterwards, their quote and their decision. The repairer's back-history answers the cold start, so day one is not empty. The intake gate answers growth, so the dataset is a function of use rather than a function of fundraising.

Three things follow.

**It is consistent with must-not 5, which is unusual.** Data minimisation and mandatory intake normally fight. Here they do not, because every field collected is a field computed on. The dishonest version of this same mechanism is everywhere; this is the honest one.

**It repairs the selection bias from the other side.** The repairer's records miss the machines nobody brought in. The user records catch them, including the people who replaced. Neither source sees the whole population and the two blind spots do not overlap — better than either alone, and still not a claim to representativeness.

**Symptoms and diagnosis stay two fields.** The user reports *what it does* — will not spin, leaks from the door, stops mid-cycle. The quote reports *what the repairer said it is*. Collapsing them lets an untrained guess enter the dataset as a diagnosis, which is must-not 6 arriving through the front door instead of the back.

**And the gate creates pressure to answer.** Someone has just typed in five facts; telling them the data is thin now feels like taking and not giving, and that is the moment a product quietly lowers its confidence threshold. The thin-data response is therefore a designed screen, not an error state: it returns the sample it does have, unweighted and labelled, and logs the request as a gap.

**Q12. Who else touches this.** The repairer. If it works they lose some jobs they would have won — specifically the ones that would have come back. That is intended, and it must be said to them rather than hidden.

---

## PART 6 — THE COMMITMENT

**Q13. Evidence the customer would accept.** They did not ring a second repairer, and twelve months later the machine is still running.

**Q14. Baseline.** Both instruments are comparisons. **MISSING** — we do not know what share of this population currently seek a second quote. **Recoverable: measure it before the build starts.**

**Q15 / Q16. Two horizons, because the instrument has two clocks.**

**Horizon A — resolvable Tuesday 15 September 2026.**
On the five most common washing-machine faults across three brands, a person can enter machine, age, symptoms and quote, and receive an answer with the reasoning visible — or be told the data is too thin to answer.

*Checked by: **nobody. This field is deliberately empty.*** No repair business has agreed to check this and none has been approached. The commitment is therefore, by the skill's own definition, not yet a commitment.

**The empty field is published as an invitation rather than an admission.** The prototype and this brief both ship in the kit. Anyone with a five-to-twelve-year-old washing machine and a quote in their hand can run the five faults and try to break it. Horizon A does not need a named checker to be resolvable — it needs a date and an artefact anyone can run.

**Horizon B — resolvable September 2027.**
Second-quote rate down against the baseline, and twelve-month survival at or above what the tool predicted.

*Checked by: **the users.*** Measured from intake records at twelve months, reported by us, evidenced by them. The person whose machine died is the one witness with no reason to soften the number, and the intake gate is what makes them reachable.

**Horizon B cannot be pulled forward and is not quietly dropped. Dropping it is how a promise becomes a feature list.**

## PART 7 — DELIBERATELY NOT SPECIFIED

Handed to the build, not decided here:

- How confidence is expressed — numbers, words, or a band.
- What the thin-data screen shows, beyond saying it is thin.
- Whether reasoning appears inline or on request.
- Interaction, layout and flow throughout.

---

## PART 8 — NOT PART OF THIS BRIEF, AND DELIBERATELY SO

**Business model, decided upstream:** commission on every completed job, repair **or** replace, so the tool earns the same either way. **That is what makes the honest answer affordable rather than heroic.** Who fulfils a replacement is an operating-model question and sits above this document.

**Strategy, business model, operating model and roadmap all precede this brief.** This document enters after them, on purpose.

## PROVENANCE

**LIVED.** The author has faced this decision repeatedly on his own fridge, dishwasher and washing machine.

Stronger than assumed. Weaker than heard. **You know the problem is real. You do not yet know that your solution preference is representative.** No interviews have been run, and nobody outside this room has said the promise sentence out loud.

One phone call to one repair business moves this to HEARD and takes thirty minutes. It was not made, and that is recorded rather than papered over.

## WHAT THE INTERVIEW CHANGED

Recorded because the point of the skill is that answering it changes the thing, not that it documents the thing.

1. **The dependency veto forced the scope down.** The promise began standing on manufacturer failure data nobody will give us. It now stands on one repairer's job history plus an intake gate. Same sentence, buildable.
2. **The selection-bias catch came from the answerer, not the questions.** Repairers only hold records on machines somebody chose to bring in. That produced must-not 4 and the log-the-declines condition — and the skill has no question that would have found it.
3. **The product moved.** It started as a cost ratio and ended as second-failure risk, because the cost ratio is arithmetic anyone can do.
4. **The mode question came back clean and still earned its place**, by returning a different answer on the same promise two days apart.
5. **Provenance gained a category.** The skill offered assumed, heard, and observed. It had no category for *I am the customer.* **LIVED** was added, with the risk written into it.
