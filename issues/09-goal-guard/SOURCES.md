# Sources

Every source below was read in full before it was used, unless it is listed under *Credited, not read*. Page numbers are the printed pages.

## The coaching method

**John Whitmore**, *Coaching for Performance: GROWing human potential and purpose. The principles and practice of coaching and leadership*, 4th edition, Nicholas Brealey Publishing, 2009 (first edition 1992). ISBN 978-1-85788-535-4. Read 4 October 2026.
Used for:
- the order of the guard's questions, from goal to first step (a short form of the GROW sequence as set out by Whitmore; he does not claim to have invented it), and his warning that the sequence "has little value" without the questions that create awareness and responsibility (p. 56);
- ownership of goals: goals handed down as imperatives deny ownership to the people expected to meet them, "and their performance is likely to suffer accordingly" (p. 61);
- "Agreed" in his goal checklist, which is why the guard does not replace agreeing your goal with your manager (p. 62);
- in the longer version tested, its own options offered only after the manager's, and only with permission (p. 82);
- the 1 to 10 commitment rating, and making a step smaller when it is rated below eight (pp. 87-88). Whitmore gives this as his practice, not as a research finding;
- telling people the "right" way keeps them dependent, and praise that replaces self-assessment increases dependence (pp. 35, 129).

## Goal research

**Edwin A. Locke and Gary P. Latham**, "Building a practically useful theory of goal setting and task motivation: A 35-year odyssey", *American Psychologist* 57(9), 2002, pp. 705-717. Read 10 October 2026.
Used for:
- an assigned goal works as well as one people help set, provided the reason for it is given; assigned tersely, it does worse (pp. 707-708);
- the main benefit of taking part in setting a goal is the information it brings out, not motivation (p. 708). This is why the guard asks: you know what the goal has to fit;
- goals with progress feedback work better than goals alone (p. 708), which is what the check-in date and the three lines are for.

**Gary P. Latham, Miriam Erez and Edwin A. Locke**, "Resolving scientific disputes by the joint design of crucial experiments by the antagonists: Application to the Erez-Latham dispute regarding participation in goal setting", *Journal of Applied Psychology* 73(4), 1988, pp. 753-772. Read 10 October 2026.
Used for: the experiments behind the point above. Assigned goals with a reason ("tell and sell") did as well as goals set together; goals assigned with brief instructions ("tell") did worse (pp. 766-768). These were students doing short brainstorming tasks where only the level of the target varied, not people choosing what to develop.

**Peter M. Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran**, "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes", *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 2006, pp. 69-119. Read 10 October 2026.
Used for: why every action gets a when and a where. Across 94 independent tests, plans that spell out "the when, where, and how" in advance had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d = .65, p. 69). The tests cover many areas of life, mostly outside work.

**Benjamin Harkin, Thomas L. Webb, Betty P. I. Chang, Andrew Prestwich, Mark Conner, Ian Kellar, Yael Benn and Paschal Sheeran**, "Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence", *Psychological Bulletin* 142(2), 2016, pp. 198-229. Read 10 October 2026.
Used for: the three lines you keep and look at again. Across 138 studies, interventions that got people to monitor their progress helped them reach their goals (d = 0.40), more so when the progress was "reported or made public" and "physically recorded" (p. 198). Almost all the studies were on health goals; only the mechanism is used here.

## Coaching and AI coaching

**Rebecca J. Jones, Stephen A. Woods and Yves R. F. Guillaume**, "The effectiveness of workplace coaching: A meta-analysis of learning and performance outcomes from coaching", *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology* 89(2), 2016, pp. 249-277. Read 10 October 2026 (early-view version).
Used for: coaching at work works. Across 17 studies, coaching by internal or external coaches had positive effects on organisational outcomes (d = 0.36), including skills and individual results.

**Erik de Haan, Nicky Terblanche and Kenneth Nowack**, "A randomised controlled comparison of the effectiveness of human and AI chatbot coaching with goal attainment, wellbeing and self-efficacy", *Human Resource Development International*, published online 28 February 2026. https://doi.org/10.1080/13678868.2026.2633990 . Read 4 October 2026.
Used for: why the guard ends with a check-in date. In this trial with 114 managers, human coaching moved their goals over one month and a purpose-built AI coach did not; many in the AI group barely used it. The AI coach was a custom chatbot, not a general assistant, and nearly all participants worked for one company. Only the goal results are used here.

**Vivienne Bihe Chi, Roman Rietsche, Andreas Göldi, Lyle Ungar and Sharath Chandra Guntuku**, "Optimized but Unowned: How AI-Authored Goals Undermine the Motivation They Are Meant to Drive", arXiv:2605.12344, version 3, 16 September 2026 (preprint). https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12344 . Read 10 October 2026.
Used for: the core reason the guard never writes your goal. In a preregistered experiment with 470 US adults, goals written by an AI from each person's own reflection were better formed, but people felt less ownership of them, and at two weeks 72.8% of those who wrote their own goals said they had acted on two or more of them, against 46.6% of those given AI-written goals. The goals were personal goals in any area of life, not work goals, and the AI wrote them in full with no editing; applying the finding to work development goals is our step, not the authors'.

**Michel Schimpf, Julian Voigt and Thomas Bohné**, "AI-Assisted Goal Setting Improves Goal Progress Through Social Accountability", arXiv:2603.17887, 18 March 2026 (preprint). https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17887 . Read 10 October 2026.
Used for: the check-ins. In a preregistered trial with 517 employed adults, an AI career coach improved two-week goal progress over no support but not over a matched written questionnaire; its distinct contribution was a greater sense of being held accountable.

## Credited, not read

**Paul Hersey and Kenneth Blanchard**, situational leadership (1969 onwards). The idea that a leader moves from coaching to delegating as a person's competence grows. The longer coaching version tested in the 54 sessions stepped back as the manager kept their commitments; that design was our own, with thresholds we chose, and it is not in the goal guard.
