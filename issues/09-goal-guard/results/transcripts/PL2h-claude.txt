That’s a really useful moment to bring, and I want to point out two things in it.

First, the fact that your team member told you directly is a good sign. People don’t usually say that to a manager they don’t trust. It means they value the time with you and believe you’ll listen. That’s worth protecting.

Second, the gap between “I thought I was on top of it” and “it’s been a month” is very common, and it’s rarely about not caring. One-to-ones are usually the most movable thing on a manager’s calendar. No customer is waiting and nothing breaks right away, so when something urgent shows up, they’re the first thing to give. Each move feels reasonable on its own. It’s only when you add them up that the pattern shows, and the person on the other side notices it much faster than you do.

So this might be less about discipline and more about how escalations reach you and what gets pushed aside when they do. That makes me curious about the escalation itself. That’s an honest and important thing to notice: it needed a decision, not you. That’s the thread worth pulling.

When a manager always says yes, the team learns, quite sensibly, that the fastest route to a decision is to bring it to you. Nobody did anything wrong. But it means you become the bottleneck for decisions, your calendar fills with other people’s urgent problems, and the slower, more important work like one-to-ones keeps losing. It can also quietly hold your team back, because they don’t get much practice making calls themselves.

I can see two goals starting to form here, and they’re connected. One is about protecting your time with your people, so one-to-ones actually happen consistently. The other is about changing how decisions reach you, so your team can handle more escalations without you and you’re pulled in only when it truly needs you. The second probably makes the first much easier, since fewer interruptions means fewer reasons to move things.

Before we shape these, I want to understand what’s really in the way. If that escalation had gone to someone on your team instead, what would have stopped them from making the decision themselves? Here are your development goals for the next 12 weeks, through mid-January 2027.

Goal 1: Make one-to-ones reliable

By mid-January, every person on my team has regular one-to-ones with me, and no one goes more than two weeks without one.

How I’ll do it: I treat one-to-ones as fixed commitments, not flexible time. If one genuinely has to move, I rebook it within the same week before the original slot passes, and never move the same one twice. Every Friday, I spend five minutes checking that every scheduled one-to-one happened.

How I’ll know it’s working: my Friday check shows no gaps, and when I ask the team in January, they say they get consistent time with me.

Goal 2: Let my team own decisions in their queues

By mid-January, my team routinely makes escalation decisions in their own queues, and I’m pulled in only when something truly needs me.

How I’ll do it: This week, I tell the team directly that decisions in their queues are theirs to make and that I’ll back them. When an escalation reaches me, my first response is “What do you think we should do?” Unless there’s serious risk to a customer or the business, I support their call, even when I’d have done it differently. If I disagree, I raise it afterwards as a learning conversation, not by overriding the decision.

How I’ll know it’s working: I keep a simple weekly tally of escalations that reach me and how many I hand back. The number reaching me should drop over the quarter, and the share I hand back should rise.

Checkpoints

In mid-November, review the Friday checks and escalation tally, and notice what’s pulling me back into old habits. In mid-December, ask one or two team members how the changes feel from their side. In mid-January, do a full review against both goals and decide what to carry into next quarter.

The two goals support each other: the more decisions your team owns, the fewer interruptions there are to push your one-to-ones aside. And the one-to-ones are where you can build the confidence that helps them decide without you.
