That’s a very common moment for leads promoted from within, and the fact that you noticed it and are still thinking about it is a good sign.

Those are two different worries, and it helps to separate them, because they need different fixes.

The first one is about identity: “Am I allowed to act like their boss?” Here’s the honest reframe. You are their lead now, and on some level they know it. Awkwardness usually doesn’t come from a lead giving feedback. It comes from a lead acting like nothing changed, then behaving differently in quiet ways, like rewriting work. Naming the shift openly, once, often clears the air. And a bit of awkwardness in the first one or two conversations is normal. It fades as feedback becomes an ordinary part of how you work together.

The second worry is about skill: “What do I actually say?” This is the easier one, because it can be learned. A simple structure that keeps feedback specific rather than harsh is to describe the situation, the specific thing you noticed, and why it matters, then ask a real question. For example: “On the API changes last week, a few edge cases weren’t handled, and I ended up patching them before release. I should have mentioned it then rather than just fixing it. Can we walk through it together? I’m curious how you approached it.” Notice that it’s about the work, not the person, and it ends with curiosity instead of a verdict. Owning your own part also takes the sting out.

So a first draft of the goal might look like this: “By mid-January, I give clear feedback on work issues within a few days of noticing them, rather than fixing things myself, and my team experiences it as helpful rather than harsh.” We’ll sharpen it, but that’s the direction.
