What you’ve just described is really useful, because each of those three thoughts is partly true. That’s why they’re so convincing in the moment.

“It’ll be faster if I do it” is usually accurate for that one instance. But it’s a short-term calculation. If the monthly report takes you two hours, that’s 24 hours a year, every year, compared with perhaps four to six hours of coaching your lead through it a couple of times. Doing it yourself wins the week, but you lose the year.

“It won’t be good enough” is worth unpacking, because it often blends two different things: work that’s genuinely below standard, and work that’s simply different from how you’d do it. The second you can let go of. The first is a real development need on your team, and the answer to that is to make your standards explicit and coach toward them, rather than take the work back. Without meaning to, taking it back keeps the gap in place.

“Deciding is the job I’m paid to do” was true at earlier stages of your career. At head-of-operations level, the job shifts toward making sure good decisions get made: setting context, clarifying who decides what, and keeping for yourself only the decisions that truly need you. Each decision you make that a lead could have made is a missed chance for them to build judgment.

Since you didn’t specify a timeframe, I’ll assume we’re planning for the rest of this year, roughly 11 weeks. Here’s a first draft of three goals built on what you’ve told me.

Hand back the monthly report for real. Return it to your team lead with a short conversation covering what “good” looks like, a deadline with some buffer built in, and an agreement that if it runs late or misses the mark, you’ll coach rather than take it over. You’ll know this goal is achieved if the October, November, and December reports are produced by your lead without you redoing them. If that goes well, pick one more recurring task to hand off in November.

Turn the Monday ops review into a place where your leads decide. Ask your leads to bring every problem along with a recommended solution, and in the meeting, ask at least one question before giving any answer. (“What would you do?” is often enough.) Within the first few weeks, sit down with your leads and agree on clear decision rights: what they decide on their own, what they decide and tell you about, and what they recommend for you to decide. Keep a simple tally each week of decisions you made versus recommendations you approved, and aim for the balance to shift visibly by December.

Protect your evenings and redirect the time. Set a concrete limit, for example leaving by a set time at least four days a week, and track how often you stay late to finish someone else’s work. Then decide in advance what the freed-up time is for, such as one strategic priority you’ve been putting off. Otherwise it tends to fill right back up.

To tie these together, I’d suggest a 10-minute reflection every Friday: Where did I step in this week, which of those three thoughts was behind it, and what will I do differently next week? That’s where the real learning will come from.
