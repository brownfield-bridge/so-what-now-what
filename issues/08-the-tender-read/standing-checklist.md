# The standing checklist

**Twelve decisions that matter most, and a full list of forty-seven behind them.**

*Four of them, D7, F5, F6 and F7, were added after the first run, because the run found things the list had no line for. That is how this file is meant to grow: from runs, not from imagination.*

This is the spine of the whole method. The guided read does not invent questions. It takes this list, reads whatever documents you point it at, and marks every line ANSWERED, PARTIAL or NOT ANSWERED with the quote and clause that settles it. You are then asked only about the gaps.

Written by a person, not by a model, so that a model cannot decide what matters.

---

## When this is worth running

**For categories you cannot replace at short notice.** High supply risk, few credible alternatives, painful to exit: what Kraljic's matrix puts in the strategic and bottleneck quadrants. There the margin for error is small, the switching cost makes leaving expensive, and an afternoon of structured reading is cheap against the cost of getting it wrong.

**Not for stationery.** If you can re-source it in a fortnight from three suppliers who all want the work, the market is your control and this is overhead.

---

## The core twelve

**Start here. If you read nothing else, decide these.**

They are the twelve that decide most disputes in a category you cannot replace quickly, and they apply whether you are buying software, logistics, a component or a service. The full list behind them exists because categories differ; this is the part that does not.

| # | Line | The decision | Where it bites |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A2 | What event starts each obligation, and what event completes it | Without both events, the two sides can measure the same commitment differently |
| 2 | A3 | What share of the volume the commitment binds | Unstated means priced as an aspiration |
| 3 | A5 | What is explicitly out of scope, named rather than inferred | Everything not excluded will be argued about |
| 4 | B1 | Whose system of record measures performance | Their reporting is them marking their own homework |
| 5 | B4 | What consequence attaches to a miss, and whether it is capped | No consequence, no commitment |
| 6 | C2 | Whether the service level survives peak, and at what volume it suspends | Peak is when you need it |
| 7 | D1 | Which standard conditions govern, and which edition | Editions differ, and the cap lives here |
| 8 | D3 | Whether consequential loss is excluded | If the loss you fear is the excluded one, the cap never comes into play |
| 9 | D7 | Whether **your own request** states your liability position, or asks for theirs | "Please state your standard terms" is a transfer, not a question |
| 10 | E3 | The indexation mechanism, with cap and floor | Uncapped indexation outlives the saving |
| 11 | G1 | How long until an alternative is operating, and who verified that rather than estimated it | This is the whole reason the category qualifies |
| 12 | H2 | Exit and transition charges, as a number or a formula | "To be agreed" is agreed at their price |

**Everything below is the full list.** It runs to forty-seven because the categories differ, not because forty-seven is a standard. Use it when the purchase is large enough to earn the hour.

## The three marks

| Mark | Means | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **ANSWERED** | The document settles it. A quote and a clause reference exist. | Recorded. You are not asked. |
| **PARTIAL** | It is discussed but not decided, or decided ambiguously. | You are asked, with the ambiguous sentence quoted back. |
| **NOT ANSWERED** | Nothing in the documents addresses it. | You are asked. |

A PARTIAL on a commitment you thought you had bought is the single most useful output of the intake.

---

## A. What is actually being bought

**A1.** What is the unit of service, defined so both parties count it the same way?

**A2.** What event starts each obligation, and what event completes it?
*Third-party logistics: does the 48 hours start at EDI receipt, at manual order entry, or at a daily cut-off time, and does it end at pick, at pack, or at carrier handover with a proof of collection? Without both events named, the two sides can measure "dispatch within 48 hours" differently and both be right.*

**A3.** What share of the volume is bound by the commitment, and what happens to the remainder?
*A service level with no stated percentage will be priced and delivered as an aspiration.*

**A4.** Which locations, entities and product groups are in scope?

**A5.** What is explicitly out of scope, named rather than left to be inferred?

**A6.** Which activities are chargeable extras rather than part of the service, and who decides which is which?

## B. Performance, and who measures it

**B1.** Which system of record produces the performance data: theirs or yours?
*If the supplier reports their own compliance from their own system, you have bought their marking of their own homework.*

**B2.** How is a disputed measurement resolved, and by whom?

**B3.** What is the reporting frequency and format, and do you receive raw records or only summaries?

**B4.** What consequence attaches to a miss (service credits, escalation, a right to withhold), and is it capped?

**B5.** What sustained level of failure gives you a right to terminate?

## C. Capacity and peak

**C1.** What volume band are the price and the service level valid for?

**C2.** Does the service level survive peak, and is there a named volume ceiling above which it suspends?

**C3.** What notice do you owe for a volume increase, and what do they owe you for a capacity constraint?

**C4.** What happens commercially if you fall below a minimum volume?

## D. Liability, risk and insurance

**D1.** Which standard conditions govern, and which edition?
*Storage is often governed by national warehousing or forwarding conditions, not by CMR, which governs international carriage of goods by road. They cap liability on different bases. Name the conditions and the edition; editions differ.*

**D2.** What is the liability cap, and expressed how: per kilogram, per event, per year, or as a multiple of fees?

**D3.** Is consequential loss excluded, and does that exclusion remove the only loss you actually care about?
*A line stoppage may well be argued to be consequential loss. If it is excluded, the cap may never come into play.*

**D4.** Who insures goods at rest, and is warehouse-keeper's liability being offered in place of all-risks cover on your stock? They are not the same thing.

**D5.** Is there a right of lien or retention over your goods in a payment dispute?
*If your stock is a production input, this can be a large exposure, and a bid will not raise it for you.*

**D6.** Who carries shrinkage and stock discrepancy, and within what tolerance?

**D7.** Does *your own* request state your liability position, or does it ask the supplier to state theirs?
*A line reading "please state your standard contract terms" is not a question. It is a transfer of the liability position to the other side of the table, and it will be accepted. This item was added after a run in which all four responses answered it with their own terms.*

## E. Commercial mechanics

**E1.** What is the charging basis for each element?
*Third-party logistics: storage per pallet in and out, per occupied location, or against a contracted minimum number of locations. The three produce very different bills at the same volume.*

**E2.** Is there a take-or-pay minimum, and what does it cost you if volumes fall?

**E3.** What is the indexation mechanism: against which published index, with what cap and what floor?

**E4.** What triggers a price review outside indexation?

**E5.** Which costs are pass-through at cost, and how is that evidenced?

## F. Data, systems and records

**F1.** What interface is committed to, who builds it, and who pays when it has to change?

**F2.** Who owns the operational data, and in what format is it returned?

**F3.** Which records must be retained, for how long, and who holds them after exit?

**F4.** Do you have access to live data, or only to reports?

**F5.** Can the supplier change the service description, support handbook or severity definitions without your agreement?
*A service level that lives in a document the supplier updates at will is not a service level.*

**F6.** Is any capability you are relying on not yet built, and if it sits on a roadmap, is the delivery date contractual?

**F7.** Can you be required to move to a new version, how long is a version supported, and who bears the cost of the move?

## G. Continuity and substitution

*This section is why the category qualifies. Everything above is contract hygiene; this is the reason a mistake here is expensive.*

**G1.** If this supplier stopped tomorrow, how long until an alternative is operating, and who has actually verified that number rather than estimated it?

**G2.** Is there a named alternative site or route, with a stated recovery time?

**G3.** Can they sub-contract the service, to whom, and do you get notice and a right of refusal?

**G4.** What single points of failure sit behind them: one site, one system, one key individual, one upstream supplier of their own?

**G5.** What must be true for you to be able to leave at all: data, stock location, tooling, intellectual property, trained people?

## H. Exit

**H1.** Notice period each way, and what triggers termination for cause?

**H2.** What are the exit and transition charges, as a number or a formula rather than "to be agreed"?

**H3.** In what condition and over what period is stock returned, and who pays for the retrieval?

**H4.** Is there an obligation to co-operate with a successor, and for how long?

## I. Governance and audit

**I1.** Who meets whom, how often, with what agenda, and who writes the minutes?

**I2.** What audit and site-access rights do you have, announced and unannounced?

**I3.** Which compliance regimes apply to these goods, and who evidences them?

**I4.** Who are the named individuals, and what happens when they leave?

---

## Point this at your own category

Four things change. Nothing else does.

1. **Section A** - the unit of service and the start and stop events for your category.
2. **The relief list seed** - what a capable supplier in *your* category would be most relieved you did not ask. This is the only part that needs your own experience, and it is the part worth most.
3. **Section D** - the standard conditions and liability basis that actually govern your trade.
4. **Section B1 and the register** - which records *you* control, because a claim you can only check from the supplier's own reporting is not checkable.

Sections C, E, F, G, H and I travel across categories almost unchanged.

---

## One note on the last count

The output that matters most is **claims that cannot be checked**: commitments that won the work and that no record you hold would ever settle. If one of them fails, nothing you hold will show it.
